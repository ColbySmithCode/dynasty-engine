# Which Pre-Draft Prospect Indicators Actually Predict Fantasy Football Success: An Evidence Review

## TL;DR
- **Draft capital is the dominant, most-replicated predictor at every skill position, and it subsumes most of the signal in college-production metrics — but not all of it.** The best-documented indicator that adds value *on top of* draft capital is early breakout age for wide receivers; athletic testing (combine/RAS) adds little once you control for where a player was drafted, with the notable exceptions of running-back speed and tight-end athleticism.
- **Almost all the fantasy-specific "evidence" is in-sample backtesting, not out-of-sample validation.** The peer-reviewed literature (Kuzmits & Adams 2008; Massey & Thaler 2013) is thin, is mostly about the combine and pick-value curves rather than fantasy points, and broadly finds combine drills weakly predictive of NFL performance. Treat published hit-rate "lifts" as backtests unless a model explicitly reports out-of-sample results.
- **Position matters: RB fantasy value is mostly opportunity/volume (so landing spot + draft capital dominate); WR is mostly talent/production (breakout age, YPRR, target share add signal); TE is the hardest to project and is best treated as a near-binary "was it a Round 1 pick?" question.** The transfer portal/NIL era (2023–2026) has degraded the cleanliness of college-production baselines, making age- and competition-adjusted metrics more important and raw dominator thresholds less reliable.

## Key Findings

1. **Draft capital is king, and the burden of proof is on any metric claiming to beat it.** JJ Zachariason's ZAP model finds the R² between draft capital and best-of-first-3-seasons PPR points (since 2000) is 0.43 for running backs. Every serious public modeler — Ryan Heath, Zachariason, the anonymous "ARM" modeler, PFF — builds draft capital in as the backbone and describes their model's job as telling you *when to deviate* from it.

2. **The "everything is draft capital" critique is largely correct but overstated.** When the ARM modeler removed draft capital, the model still found real (if smaller) signal in breakout age, BMI/size, target share, and rushing usage. Collinearity is the central problem: production and draft capital are correlated, so production's apparent effect shrinks sharply once draft position is controlled.

3. **Breakout age is the single best-supported "adds-value-on-top-of-draft-capital" indicator for WRs** — but the evidence is backtested, not out-of-sample. Peter Howard (Dynasty League Football, April 2018) found: "Since 2001, 73% of all players who have been drafted in the first round with a breakout age of 18 have become top 24 players in PPR scoring" — roughly 14 percentage points above the first-round baseline of ~59%.

4. **The NFL Combine / athletic testing is weakly predictive of NFL performance once draft position is accounted for, with position-specific exceptions.** Kuzmits & Adams (2008) found no consistent combine-to-performance relationship except RB sprint speed. RAS mostly predicts what teams *do* (who they draft), not who succeeds.

5. **Landing spot matters more for RBs than WRs.** The maxim "talent matters most at WR; opportunity matters most at RB" is well supported: a modestly talented RB with volume produces, while WR production requires the player to earn targets by getting open.

6. **TE is the hardest position to project, and the practical rule is nearly binary: bet on Round 1 TEs, be very skeptical otherwise** — though athleticism and receiving-style production help.

## Details by Indicator

### 1. College Dominator Rating (share of team receiving/rushing yards and TDs)
**Origin.** Popularized by RotoViz's Frank DuPont, Shawn Siegele, and Jon Moore (~2013–2014); operationalized by PlayerProfiler (Matt Kelley), which sets the WR breakout threshold at 20% dominator, and 15% for RB/TE. For WR/TE it's the share of team receiving production; for RB it's share of total offensive (rush+receiving) production.

**What held up.** Dominator rating is a real signal — but largely because it is a component of breakout age and target/market share, and because it correlates with draft capital. Backtests consistently show breakout WRs posted high peak dominators (an Apex Fantasy Leagues study reported a ~33.6% average peak dominator among rookie WR breakouts, though that exact figure could not be independently re-verified on the page). The clearer Apex finding: 83.9% of rookie WR breakouts were drafted in Rounds 1–2, and "Zero rookies selected after Round 5 have broken out in the past 12 years, with Puka Nacua becoming just the second 5th-rounder to do so."

**Skeptical case.** Threshold arbitrariness (why 20%?), no opponent adjustment (a 200-yard game vs. an FCS school counts the same as vs. an SEC defense — a point made explicitly by the Northwestern Sports Analytics group), and collinearity with draft capital. In the transfer-portal/NIL era, dominator baselines are distorted because rosters churn and older transfers concentrate production.

**Verdict.** Useful as an *input* to age-adjusted production and market share, but weak as a standalone once you control for draft capital and competition level. Held up as a building block, not as an independent predictor.

### 2. Breakout Age / Age-Adjusted Production
**Origin.** Coined by Shawn Siegele (RotoViz, 2014), building on DuPont and Jon Moore; extended by Anthony Amico (regression-tree analysis, 2018) and Peter Howard (breakout age by draft round, DLF 2018), and popularized by PFF's Scott Barrett ("skeleton key"). Definition: age at the start of the season a player first hits a 20% dominator (WR).

**What held up — the strongest incremental-value evidence in the whole space.** Peter Howard's backtest (WRs drafted since 2001): "73% of all players who have been drafted in the first round with a breakout age of 18 have become top 24 players in PPR scoring," and "in the first round, the drop off in hit rate from 18 to 21 is steep." There is a clean monotonic decline in hit rate by breakout age across every draft round. Of 72 WRs since 2001 drafted outside the first four rounds with a breakout age over 19, only 3 became top-24 PPR players. Adam Harstad's a priori rationale: dominating while age-disadvantaged against older defenders is itself a talent signal.

**Skeptical case.** All backtested; no published out-of-sample ΔR². Non-monotonicity at ages 21–22 betrays small samples. "Early declare" status is confounded with talent and draft capital. Transfer portal/NIL now keep players in college longer and inflate late-breakout counts, potentially eroding the cutoffs. Threshold arbitrariness again (age 20 as a hard line).

**Verdict.** The best-supported production indicator with apparent incremental value over draft capital for WRs — but the effect is a within-tier hit-rate lift from backtests, not a validated out-of-sample regression gain. Trust it as a tiebreaker within a draft round, not as an override of draft capital.

### 3. College Target/Market Share & Air Yards (YPTPA, air yards share, YPRR)
**Origin.** Market share metrics from RotoViz (DuPont/Siegele); yards per team pass attempt (YPTPA/YPTA) and market share of receiving yards (MSRY) via Campus2Canton; air yards from Josh Hermsmeyer (creator of WOPR/RACR, airyards.com); yards per route run (YPRR) heavily used by PFF, Ryan Heath, and the Elite Drafters model.

**What held up.** YPRR is described by PFF as one of the most stable college-to-NFL and year-to-year receiving metrics, and it's a backbone of multiple current models. Target share has a "Goldilocks" nonlinearity in the ARM model — 15–20% is ideal, while >25% often flags a weak surrounding cast / small-school inflation. Hermsmeyer's air-yards work showed opportunity metrics (aDOT, air-yards share) predict receiving volume far better than efficiency metrics, which regress to the mean ("the greatest trick the devil ever pulled was convincing fantasy footballers that efficiency is useful… when you test them to see if they predict anything, you always find that they come crashing back to the mean").

**Skeptical case.** Target/market share is heavily context-dependent (scheme, QB, teammates). YPRR vs. zone/man splits are being over-interpreted in the current cycle (the "Denzel Boston / KC Concepcion low-YPRR-vs-zone = bust" discourse that Jakob Sanderson criticizes as "some folks in the data-based prospecting space [having] lost the plot"). Small-school inflation and lack of opponent adjustment persist. Collinearity with draft capital remains.

**Verdict.** YPRR and air-yards-share are the most defensible production metrics — genuinely stable and predictive of opportunity — but they still largely operate *within* the draft-capital baseline. Air yards' biggest validated win is in-season volume prediction, not pre-draft projection.

### 4. Athletic Testing Composites (Combine, RAS, SPARQ, Speed Score, 40, etc.)
**Origin.** Football Outsiders' Speed Score (Bill Barnwell, Pro Football Prospectus 2008) for RBs (weight × 40-time; average NFL RB ≈ 100, elite threshold ~110); Playmaker Score (2009) for WRs; RAS (Relative Athletic Score, Kent Lee Platte, 2013).

**Peer-reviewed evidence.** Kuzmits & Adams (2008, *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* 22(6):1721–1727), covering QBs/RBs/WRs drafted 1999–2004: "Using correlation analysis, we find no consistent statistical relationship between combine tests and professional football performance, with the notable exception of sprint tests for running backs." Only RB 40-time correlated with early-career salary, games played, and yards per carry; QBs and WRs showed no combine-to-success correlation. Corroborated by Robbins (2010), Teramoto et al. (2016), and LaPlaca & McCullick (2020). Tucker & Black (2021, *The Sport Journal*) found combine performance predicts *draft status* only weakly and mostly for WRs (WR 40-yard dash Spearman r ≈ .44), essentially zero for RBs (r ≈ .008) and QBs — i.e., the combine reflects what teams do more than who succeeds.

**Fantasy-community evidence.** Anthony Amico and Establish the Run: athletic measurables usually don't appear as significant in WR models — "a lot of the signal likely being covered by other inputs" — though crude thresholds exist. RAS creator Platte himself concedes RAS is "indicative of how teams like to roster build, but not necessarily success."

**Position exception — TE.** Ryan Heath's TE work finds athleticism matters "a ton at the TE position, even above and beyond what draft capital tells us" — the clearest case where testing adds incremental value.

**Verdict.** Athletic testing is the most overrated indicator relative to its fantasy-community prominence. It mostly predicts draft capital, not success, for WR/QB. Real, validated exceptions: RB speed (peer-reviewed) and TE athleticism (analyst-backtested).

### 5. Draft Capital
**Origin/thesis.** "Draft capital is king." Baseline for essentially every model.

**Evidence.** RB draft-capital→PPR R² = 0.43 (ZAP, backtest). Hit-rate gradients are steep: Round 1 WRs hit top-12 ~34.6% / top-24 ~48.7%; Round 2 ~22.7% top-12; Round 3 ~11.7% top-12. TEs: ~10 of 11 first-round TEs since 2016 posted at least one TE1 season (O.J. Howard the lone exception); Day 2 TEs ~27% TE1 hit rate; over the 2016–2025 decade only 6% of all drafted TEs finished as top-12. Massey & Thaler's "Loser's Curse" (*Management Science* 59(7):1479–1495, 2013): "We find that top draft picks are significantly overvalued in a manner that is inconsistent with rational expectations and efficient markets, and consistent with psychological research" (10th pick valued ~39% of the 1st in trades). Brill & Wyner (2024, arXiv:2411.10400) found the surplus-value curve replicates out-of-sample on 2013–2023 data — one of the few genuinely out-of-sample confirmations in the literature — but argue "their conclusion hinges on the assumption that general managers should use expected surplus value as their utility function," and that the loser's curse breaks down for elite QBs.

**Skeptical case.** Draft capital is itself a noisy human forecast; it can be wrong (Puka Nacua, 5th round; Tyreek Hill's off-field slide). It partly reflects landing-spot opportunity rather than pure talent. Round-1 WR "hit" rates for elite (top-5/WR1) outcomes are actually low (~20.5%), so draft capital is better at avoiding busts than guaranteeing stars.

**Verdict.** The most robust single predictor, and the correct Bayesian prior. But it is a prior, not destiny — the entire value of production models is in identifying the ~10–20% of cases where the market mispriced a player.

### 6. Landing Spot / Team Context
**Evidence.** The "talent vs. opportunity" decomposition: opportunity/volume dominates RB fantasy value (snaps → touches → points), while WR value requires talent to earn targets. As one FantasyPros analysis put it, "Talent matters most at WR; opportunity matters most at RB… Most any NFL running back can provide fantasy value if he's getting 15 or more carries per game and his offensive line is creating running room." This is why RB rankings swing hardest on landing spot and why analysts (e.g., Jakob Sanderson) explicitly withhold RB rankings until after the draft. For WRs, landing spot matters less than commonly believed — elite talents (Justin Jefferson behind Adam Thielen, Ja'Marr Chase alongside Tee Higgins, Brandon Aiyuk with Deebo Samuel) produced despite crowded depth charts.

**Verdict.** Landing spot is real and is *most* important for RBs and for Year-1 usage across positions. For long-run WR value it is secondary to talent. The common error is over-weighting "too many mouths to feed" narratives for WRs and under-weighting scheme/volume for RBs.

## Position-Specific Summary
- **QB:** College projection is genuinely hard. The Lewin Career Forecast (David Lewin, Football Outsiders, 2006) used games started + completion percentage and worked in-sample (pre-2005) but failed out-of-sample after 2006 (flagged busts Leinart, Quinn, Brohm, Clemens) — a textbook cautionary tale of a backtested model that broke on new data. Football Outsiders itself later conceded the metric "is more descriptive than it is predictive." Modern fantasy QB value is dominated by rushing ability; college rushing production and early starting are the most fantasy-relevant signals (recent top-12 rookie QBs — Daniels, Nix, Stroud, Murray — nearly all rushed and started Week 1).
- **RB:** Draft capital + opportunity dominate. RotoViz's Prospect Lab (a pre-draft, no-draft-position linear model) still identifies the true elites (Barkley 100, Taylor 97, McCaffrey 93) via age, weight, 40, three-cone, competition, and dominator. Speed Score (RB 40 × weight) has the only peer-reviewed athletic-testing support. Zachariason found rushing-production metrics add little; receiving profile matters more for fantasy.
- **WR:** The position where production models add the most. Breakout age, YPRR, target/market share, air-yards share, and early-declare status all carry (backtested) signal beyond draft capital. But high-end (WR1) outcomes remain hard to predict even for Round 1 picks.
- **TE:** Hardest to project. Near-binary on Round 1 capital; athleticism uniquely matters beyond draft capital; receiving-style ("WR test") production profiles drive the recent hits (LaPorta, Bowers, Warren, Fannin). Very low deep-target share is a red flag (Ryan Heath: of 29 TEs since 2015 with <10% of receiving yards on deep passes, only 2 posted a 600+ yard season).

## Recommendations
1. **Start every prospect evaluation from draft capital as the prior.** For redraft/dynasty, do not override draft capital on the strength of college stats alone; use production to rank *within* draft tiers and to find mispriced players post-draft (the demonstrated edge is on waivers/rookie-draft value, per ARM and ZAP).
2. **For WRs, weight breakout age and YPRR/air-yards-share highest among production metrics**, but treat the specific thresholds (age 20, 20% dominator) as soft, not hard — especially given transfer-portal/NIL distortions. Apply a discount to production accumulated against Group-of-5 competition and to late-breakout profiles outside the first four rounds (near-zero historical hit rate: 3 of 72).
3. **For RBs, prioritize projected volume/landing spot and draft capital; use Speed Score and receiving profile as tiebreakers.** Wait until after the NFL Draft to finalize RB rankings.
4. **For TEs, bet on Round 1 capital and elite athleticism; be highly skeptical of Day 2–3 TEs regardless of college production.** Prefer receiving-style TEs with real deep-target share.
5. **Discount athletic testing (RAS/SPARQ/40) for WR and QB.** Use it mainly to confirm RB speed and TE athleticism, and to understand why a player was drafted where he was — not as an independent success predictor.
6. **Benchmarks that would change these recommendations:** a published *out-of-sample* model showing production adds a meaningful ΔR² over draft capital across 3+ future classes; evidence that transfer-portal/NIL has broken the breakout-age cutoffs (watch the 2024–2027 classes); or a demonstrated shift in RB workload structure (return of bell-cows) that would further re-elevate RB draft capital.

## Caveats
- **Backtest vs. out-of-sample:** The overwhelming majority of fantasy-community findings (dominator, breakout age, market share hit rates, ZAP's 0.43 R², Speed Score) are in-sample backtests. No peer-reviewed, out-of-sample study was found that isolates a clean ΔR² for "draft capital + production vs. draft capital alone" in fantasy points. The only genuinely out-of-sample confirmations found were the Massey–Thaler pick-value curve (Brill & Wyner 2024) and, negatively, the Lewin Career Forecast's post-2006 failure. The "ARM" model is a rare fantasy example that explicitly tests on held-out 2021–2023 classes (reporting R² improving from ~0.30 to ~0.47), which is why it deserves more weight than typical backtest-only claims.
- **Defining "success" is unresolved and drives conclusions.** Top-24 seasons, PPR points per game, career value, and hit-rate thresholds give different answers; hit-rate framing (used by most cited work) rewards floor over ceiling and can make draft capital look more decisive than a points-per-game or ceiling-based definition would.
- **Collinearity/p-hacking risk is pervasive.** Many "significant" thresholds come from multiple-comparison searches on small samples (a few hundred WRs, ~130 TEs since 2018/2019). Effect sizes should be treated as upper bounds.
- **Era effects are large and growing.** Rising college passing volume, spread offenses, the transfer portal (roughly 25–30% of scholarship football players entered the portal in the most recent cycle), and NIL have all shifted production baselines since the foundational 2013–2018 research. Metrics validated on 2000–2015 classes may not transfer cleanly.
- **Some cited outlets are marketing their own products** (BrainyBallers SPS, PlayerProfiler, Elite Drafters, ZAP, PFF). Their self-reported accuracy claims (e.g., "proven to have a higher accuracy than draft capital alone") should be treated skeptically absent independent out-of-sample validation. BrainyBallers' repeated "Pearson value" claims in particular conflate weak correlations (|r| ≈ 0.14–0.27) with "strong" relationships and should not be taken at face value.
