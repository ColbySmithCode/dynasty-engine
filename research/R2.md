# The Dynasty Analytics Debates: Competing Frameworks for Valuing Rookie Picks and Aging Players

## TL;DR
- The serious dynasty analytics community has **not** converged on how to value rookie picks or aging players; it has split into durable camps — market/crowdsourced (KeepTradeCut), revealed-preference (FantasyCalc), and expert-model (DynastyProcess) for picks; and "aging curve" vs. Adam Harstad's "mortality table" framing for players — and the most interesting disagreements persist because they rest on *different defensible assumptions*, not on contested facts.
- The sharpest live fault lines are: (1) whether the community systematically overprices early rookie 1sts relative to realized hit rates (the first round hits at only ~47.6%) the way NFL GMs overprice top NFL picks (Massey-Thaler); (2) whether the RB "age cliff" at 26-27 is real biological decline or a survivorship/usage artifact; and (3) whether static trade calculators are broken by context-blindness and reflexivity or are simply imperfect-but-useful negotiation anchors.
- Where consensus *does* exist: future picks are discounted (~20%/year or "one round per year" is the common heuristic, though a minority calls this an exploitable inefficiency); superflex dramatically inflates QB and early-pick value; and RB value decays earlier and faster than WR value — even the skeptics mostly argue about *why* and *how much*, not *whether*.

## Key Findings
1. **Rookie pick valuation is contested along a market-vs-model axis.** Three tools embody three philosophies: KeepTradeCut (crowdsourced pairwise "Keep/Trade/Cut" votes run through an adapted Elo), FantasyCalc (values derived from millions of real executed trades), and DynastyProcess (an exponential-decay transform of FantasyPros Expert Consensus Rankings). They disagree most on future picks and on studs-vs-depth.
2. **The Massey-Thaler "Loser's Curse" logic partially transfers but is contested.** The academic finding that NFL top picks are overvalued on a *surplus-value* basis has an obvious dynasty analog (mediocre rookie-pick hit rates), but the transfer is imperfect because dynasty has no rookie salary scale — the central mechanism of the NFL surplus argument.
3. **The RB age cliff is the community's most heated aging debate.** "Sell RBs by 26" is near-orthodoxy, but a serious skeptical camp (led by Adam Harstad) argues the cliff is an artifact of aggregation, survivorship, and usage rather than smooth biological decline.
4. **Discount rates for future picks are a genuine philosophical split.** "One round per year" / ~20% per year is dominant, but a contrarian camp argues future picks are systematically underpriced (optionality/arbitrage), mirroring an identical NFL debate.
5. **Contend-vs-rebuild frameworks converge on "avoid the middle" but lack empirical backing.** The "no man's land" argument is widely shared, but there is a striking absence of published data on how often rebuilds actually succeed or what age title-winning rosters are.

## Details

### AREA 1 — Competing Frameworks for Rookie Pick Valuation

**The three market/tool philosophies (and how they differ mechanically):**

- **KeepTradeCut (KTC):** Purely crowdsourced. Users are shown three players/picks and rank them Keep/Trade/Cut; KTC, in its own words, uses "an adapted ELO algorithm" to produce values on a ~0-10,000 scale. Strength: fast-moving, reflects live community sentiment. Weakness: it *is* community sentiment — it measures the crowd, and is widely described as youth-weighted. KTC also requires the user to guess whether a future pick lands early or late in its round.
- **FantasyCalc:** Derived from real completed trades ("millions of real fantasy football trades... run through an optimization algorithm," updated multiple times daily; self-reported trade counts have ranged from ~2.6M to ~3.6M). Built by two brothers (a data scientist and a data engineer) since 2020. Distinctive result: FantasyCalc "greatly devalues future picks... because it's agnostic as to where they'll end up" (thirdc.substack.com). This is revealed preference — what people actually *do*, not what they *say*.
- **DynastyProcess (DP):** Built by Tan Ho and Joe Sydlowski; open-source. Values are a deterministic transform of FantasyPros Dynasty ECR: `Value = 10500 * e^(FP ECR * -0.0235)`. The decay coefficient is user-tunable (−0.0220 values depth more; −0.0250 values studs more), directly operationalizing the studs-vs-depth debate. Rookie picks blend two GAM models ("Perfect Knowledge" and "Hit Rate," preset 80/20); future picks are set to 80% of current-year value (a flat 20% annual discount). DP explicitly acknowledges its core assumption — "we are assuming that FantasyPros Expert Consensus Rankings are an accurate way to determine how players are valued long-term" — and even nods to a better possible method: "expected value over replacement as suggested by Adam Harstad."

**Outcome-based / expected-value approaches:** A separate camp values picks by realized hit rates rather than market price, and the numbers are sobering. Per NBC Sports' "By the Numbers" study of 504 rookie picks (six-round, 12-team PPR dynasty drafts): "91/504 (18%), or close to 1 out of every 5.5 players registered at least one season in top 12 QB/TE or top 24 RB/WR fantasy scorers. 30/504 (6%) of those did it during their rookie season." The same study found "the first round has delivered a 47.6% hit rate... This has trended to seven of 12 hits over the examined time period, or 58% success," while "roughly one of three second-round picks, or 31%, have produced at least one countable season since 2010" (and even the first-round rate drops to ~30% once one-season-only busts like Jahvid Best, C.J. Spiller, Trent Richardson, and RGIII are excluded). Matt Mesisca's 2012-2017 study (building on RotoViz's Jacob Rickrode) found similar steep round-over-round decay; Garret Price's 2018-2023 data (used by Dynasty Nerds) shows the 1.01 with a high "hit or mid" rate that justifies a premium. The camp's takeaway: because hit rates fall off a cliff after the top few picks, mid-first and second-round picks are lottery tickets, and the market's willingness to tear down rosters for them is often irrational.

**The Massey-Thaler transfer:** Cade Massey and Richard Thaler's "The Loser's Curse" (*Management Science*, 2013) found NFL teams systematically overvalue top draft picks: top picks produce *less surplus value* (on-field value minus salary cost) than later first-rounders, and the trade-market (Jimmy Johnson) curve is far steeper than the surplus-value curve. As Ryan Brill et al. summarize in "The Winner of the NFL Draft is Not Necessarily Cursed" (arXiv, 2024): "The trade market curve is much steeper than expected performance and surplus value curves. In other words, teams aren't valuing draft picks by their expected value... even teams at the very top of the draft should trade down." Dynasty writers explicitly invoke this, framing KTC as the dynasty equivalent of the Jimmy Johnson chart — the steep "gold standard" everyone trades against. But the transfer is contested on a crucial point: the NFL surplus argument depends on the rookie wage scale (cheap labor cost of a pick), which has *no dynasty analog* — dynasty rookies cost only the pick, not a salary. The 2024 arXiv companion paper ("The Loser's Curse and the Critical Role of the Utility Function") further argues the "curse" hinges on assuming GMs *should* maximize expected surplus value; allow a variance-seeking utility function and top picks can be rational — a critique that maps neatly onto the dynasty "lottery ticket / optionality" defense of early picks.

**Where they disagree most sharply:**
- *Early 1sts vs. bundles of mid picks:* Outcome/EV analysts and consolidation-aware voices argue a single elite young asset beats a bundle because roster spots are scarce and only starters score; KTC/market values, taken literally, will sometimes call "12 third-round picks for DeAndre Hopkins" fair (KTC applies a "value adjustment" specifically to counter this).
- *Systematic overpricing of picks:* The "productive struggle"/EV camp says yes, the community overpays for hope; the market camp says price *is* value by definition.
- *The rookie-pick premium / optionality:* A pick is worth more than the player it becomes the moment it's used — DP's "Perfect Knowledge" model is explicitly designed to capture "the absolute maximum a pick should be worth" because a pick "loses value the moment it is converted into a rookie player." This flexibility premium is the strongest argument *for* the market's pick-love.

**Format sensitivity (broad consensus, little debate):** Superflex dramatically raises QB and early-1st value — "elite QBs become the most valuable dynasty assets, period," because ~24 starting slots chase ~32 startable QBs. In superflex rookie drafts, QBs vault up boards and skill players fall (a documented CBS mock had Round-1-caliber players sliding to Round 3). TE-premium and full-PPR raise TE/receiver values; KTC and DP both expose format toggles. Devy/future-class picks add another discount layer (see Area 3).

### AREA 2 — Positional Aging Curves and the RB Age Cliff

**The orthodox "cliff" camp:** The dominant view holds RBs decline sharply around 26-27. Representative claims: DLF found that over 2019-2021, just two RBs over age 26 posted a top-12 season (an "immediate cliff dive" after 25-26); peak-age studies put the modern RB peak at ~24.8-25.5 and find 93.8% of peak RB seasons occur before age 29; Rich Hribar's 2000-2020 study found only 16.3% of top-24 RB seasons came from RBs 29+. Practical prescription: "sell a year early, not a year late." WR is treated as a longer, later curve (peak ~26-27, meaningful production into the early 30s; PFF's "Age of Decline" work and Fantasy Footballers' lifecycle studies put WR1 peak at ~26). TE is the "slow developer" (Year-2 breakouts a "cheat code"; but Fantasy Points notes that stripping the top-5 all-time outliers drops average TE peak to ~26.8 — i.e., survivorship inflates the "TEs age well" narrative). QB ages best of all — Brian Burke's early work showed QB curves flatten to a "stable plateau" once you drop the final season.

**The skeptical camp (Adam Harstad, Footballguys) — the strongest challenge:** Harstad's evergreen "We're Probably Thinking about Age the Wrong Way" (2015) argues the aging *curve* is the wrong model. His thought experiment: even players with zero true decline will *look* like a smooth curve in aggregate if a rising share suffer "career-ending" events each year (the "witch doctor"/ping-pong-ball model). He proposes "mortality tables" instead: model the *probability* a player falls off a cliff ("dies"), while survivors hold roughly steady. His evidence, pulling "the top 50 running backs and the top 50 wide receivers of the last 30 years in career fantasy value, looking only at retired players" (defining the last relevant season as the last year above 20 EVoB): exactly 50% declined in their final fantasy-relevant season (not the >50% a curve predicts), and survivor production is nearly flat for WRs (RBs show a mild pre-cliff dip). Upshot: aging is "a series of coin flips" weighted more toward tails with age — every player is a risk of degree, and dramatic busts arrive "without forewarning." Harstad also argues (in "You're Older Than You've Ever Been") that "most owners are too willing to sell low on aging studs when they rebuild."

**The methodological core of the fight (genuinely unresolved):** All aging-curve estimation suffers from:
- *Survivorship/selection bias:* You can't measure aging in a population that gets cut for being bad — surviving old players are a filtered elite, so raw curves understate decline among "survivors" (Fantasy Points flags this explicitly, citing Harstad, with the Ezekiel Elliott example: a player who washes out simply exits the sample).
- *The delta method and its corrections:* Imported from baseball (Tom Tango) and hockey (EvolvingWild/Younggren twins; CJ Turtoro's stress tests), the delta method averages year-over-year changes for players in consecutive seasons — but is biased because unlucky players don't return to "regress back up." Mitchel Lichtman's fix adds "phantom" seasons (projected performance for non-returnees). There is a live academic disagreement: JC Bradbury's regression approach put peak age ~29, while delta-method adherents (Lichtman) put it earlier; *Baseball Prospectus*'s "The Delta Method, Revisited" warns that removing final seasons (Tango's method) may *over*-correct and understate real decline. This baseball/hockey methods war is the substrate football analysts borrow from, and it has not been settled.

**Age vs. mileage:** A distinct sub-debate. The "mileage" camp (touch counts predict breakdown — e.g., decline after ~1,500-1,800 or ~2,250-2,500 career touches) is intuitive and widely cited. Harstad flatly rejects it in "Age vs. Mileage" (2022): using Josh Jacobs vs. Rhamondre Stevenson (12 days apart in age but 1,176 vs. 390 career touches), he argues "mileage" is "predictively meaningless" and that *age*, not touches, predicts remaining career. Others (Neil Paine's commenters; various workload studies) hold the opposite. Methodological weakness on the mileage side: high-touch backs are *selected* for being good enough to earn touches, confounding touches with talent/opportunity — and the studies rarely disentangle this.

**Draft capital / talent as the real driver:** A third position holds that draft capital and talent, not age per se, dominate — teams stop feeding older backs (opportunity), and recency bias in draft capital drives usage. This reframes the "cliff" as a *usage* phenomenon (committees, teams moving on) rather than a physical one.

### AREA 3 — Discount Rates for Future Assets

**The dominant heuristic:** "Subtract one round per year" (Footballguys' Pasquino) and/or ~10-30% per year. DynastyProcess bakes in a flat 20% (future pick = 80% of current-year value). Arguments for a *high* discount: uncertainty about draft slot, roster churn, league instability/folding risk, win-now leverage, and the plain fact a pick helps you sooner.

**The contrarian "future picks are underpriced" camp:** An empirical Footballguys forum analysis argued the market *overstates* the drop-off — even at a 50% discount, a future first slides only ~a round versus this year's equivalent, implying future firsts are often a bargain. FantasyPros and others advise routinely pushing traded picks to future years to harvest this discount. This mirrors an *identical* NFL debate: a Substack analysis ("NFL Teams are Undervaluing Future Year Draft Picks") argues the one-round-per-year convention is unjustified because the rookie wage scale is cap-tied (no inflation) and picks can't be "invested," so standard time-value-of-money logic doesn't apply. The empirical anchor for this camp: Massey & Thaler, using a dataset of 407 draft-day trades, found NFL "teams discount the future at an extraordinary rate (136 percent)" — a pick worth 100 this year valued at only ~64 next year — wildly steeper than finance would justify.

**The reflexivity caveat on NPV framing:** Applying finance-style net-present-value math to fantasy assets is contested because (unlike dollars) picks can't be reinvested, and the "currency" (player values) is itself set by the same market you're trading in.

**Harstad's reframing of the discount question:** In "The Difference Between Contending and Rebuilding," Harstad casts dynasty as a Blotto-style resource-allocation game and argues "the difference between rebuilding teams and contending teams is that contending teams have a steeper time discount" — a contender might discount each future season ~25%, a balanced team ~10%, and a rebuilder might have a *negative* discount (valuing future seasons *more*). This recasts the discount rate as a *strategic choice* dependent on your window, not a universal constant — a pointed critique of one-size-fits-all pick-discount charts.

### AREA 4 — Contend-vs-Rebuild Decision Frameworks

**The "no man's land" consensus:** Nearly every serious writer warns against the middle. DLF: being "stuck in the middle... good enough to avoid premium rookie picks but not good enough to seriously threaten for a title" is "one of the most dangerous places to be." Footballguys' lifecycle framework urges an honest projected-record ranking and commitment to extremes. Recurring theme: don't "fence sit."

**Championship-equity frameworks:** The rigorous version asks not "can I make the playoffs?" but "can I realistically win a title, and does this move meaningfully change that probability?" DynastyProcess's "Crystal Ball" uses All-Play win% as a contend/rebuild proxy. Fantasy Football Foundry articulates the key limit of calculators here: "these calculations become less useful the closer a team gets to a championship" — a contender should rationally make trades that "look inefficient in the market but [are] perfectly rational for the roster," because only starters score and hoarded value that never enters the lineup is wasted.

**The empirical vacuum (important, genuinely unresolved):** Despite confident strategic writing, there is a striking absence of public, rigorous data on (a) how often rebuilds actually win a title within N years, and (b) whether championship rosters skew young or veteran. The best available evidence is anecdotal (Harstad's self-reported n=1 rebuild that went 148-39 in all-play) or is *player-level* production age (top-12 WRs average ~26.5, top-12 TEs ~27) rather than *title-team* age. The contend-vs-rebuild debate is therefore argued largely on theory and vibes, not outcome data — a weakness the community rarely admits (DLF and Dynasty Nerds both concede "there is no simple formula").

**Stars-and-scrubs vs. depth:** A live roster-construction split. The "stars" camp says concentrate value in elite studs (only starters score; scarcity at the top wins weeks, especially QB/TE in superflex/TEP). The "depth" camp notes dynasty is a war of attrition with thin waiver wires, so bench depth and surviving injuries win titles. Format dependence is decisive: deeper benches, larger leagues, and taxi squads reward depth and patience; shallow leagues reward concentration. Several writers note the market *systematically overvalues youth and undervalues proven veterans*, making win-now veteran buys a market inefficiency for contenders.

**League-format dependence:** Playoff structure, roster size, taxi squads, number of teams, and especially draft-order rules that punish tanking all shift the optimal strategy.

### AREA 5 — Critiques of Static Trade Calculators

**The strongest structural critiques:**
- *Context-blindness:* Values are set "in a vacuum" (KTC's own instruction to voters) and ignore roster construction, team window, positional need, scoring, and starter requirements. A DLF forum thread: "values of players are heavily dependent on starting requirements, league size, roster size, and number of teams," none of which a raw number captures.
- *The consolidation / 2-for-1 problem:* Because only starters score and roster spots are scarce, calculators that sum parts systematically misprice consolidation. KTC explicitly reverse-engineers a "value adjustment" to avoid saying "12 third round picks are a fair deal for DeAndre Hopkins" — an admission naive summation is broken. Javelin Fantasy Football's teardown details KTC's hidden "raw adjustment" (roughly 10-40% of a player's value) used to balance multi-player packages.
- *Reflexivity/circularity:* KTC's values are set by the same people who consult KTC, so the tool can *manufacture* the consensus it claims to measure — a genuine epistemic problem for crowdsourced tools that afflicts FantasyCalc (revealed trades) and DP (expert rankings) less, though DP inherits whatever biases live in FantasyPros ECR.
- *Lag, recency bias, and herding:* Forum consensus is that calculators are "overreactive" to news/injury and lag true value, creating buy-low/sell-high opportunities against the tool.

**The methodological critique of each source:** KTC (pairwise crowd votes → Elo) is fast but circular and youth-skewed; FantasyCalc (real trades) captures behavior but is thin on and "agnostic" about future picks and can encode desperation/collusion trades; DP (FantasyPros ECR → decay curve) is transparent and tunable but only as good as expert consensus and updates as slowly as ECR does. The thirdc.substack analysis used FantasyCalc's own trade DB to show sellers of current-year thirds "win" the trade only ~a quarter of the time and early seconds ~a third — evidence the market itself misprices the picks the calculators are built on.

**The counterargument (calculators are fine, critics overstate it):** KTC's own framing — "calculators are at best a gut check, so we aim to be the best gut check we can be" — is echoed by pros who treat them as a *common language / negotiation anchor* rather than an oracle. Ben Gretch points people to RotoViz's tiering (which relates every player back to rookie-pick equivalents) as a better mental model but still uses value tooling. Pragmatic consensus: the tools are indispensable for getting a deal in the ballpark and terrible for finalizing contender/consolidation decisions — and sophisticated managers exploit the gap between the tool and reality.

## Recommendations

**For valuing rookie picks — triangulate, don't anchor.** Use all three tool philosophies together and treat divergence as signal: when FantasyCalc (real trades) prices a future pick well below KTC (crowd sentiment), that gap *is* the market's future-pick discount, and you can arbitrage it. Anchor on hit-rate reality (first round ~47.6%, falling to ~31% by the second round) rather than on hope. Escalate toward paying up for early 1sts only when you have a concrete starter-lineup use for the resulting player and a contention window open within one year.

**For aging players — hold both models.** Treat "sell RBs by 26" as a strong prior but apply Harstad's mortality-table lens: don't assume smooth decline, assume rising cliff-risk with steady survivor production. Concrete trigger to sell an RB: age 26-27 *plus* any erosion in role/opportunity — the usage-driven decline is more predictable than the physical one. Do NOT use career touches as a primary sell signal (weak predictor, confounded by talent). For WR/QB/TE, be materially more patient; the sell-early logic is much weaker off the RB curve.

**For future picks — set your discount by your window, not by a chart.** If you're a genuine contender, a steep discount (~25%+/yr) on future picks is rational; if you're rebuilding, discount them little or not at all and actively buy future picks at the market's ~20%/yr discount. The benchmark that should flip you from rebuild to contend: a realistic top-3 projected roster in your specific league, not a playoff-bubble roster.

**For contend-vs-rebuild — pick an extreme and commit.** Avoid the middle. Because the empirical success-rate data doesn't exist, treat this as risk management: the downside of perpetual mediocrity is worse than the variance of a committed teardown or all-in push. Re-evaluate at the trade deadline and post-NFL-draft each year.

**For calculators — use as anchor, override for context.** Use KTC/FantasyCalc/DP to get within range and to speak a common language with trade partners; override them for consolidation trades, contender win-now buys, and anything sensitive to your starter requirements. The moment a trade is "clean on the calculator" but doesn't change your starting lineup, distrust the calculator.

## Caveats
- **Genuinely unresolved (not just unsettled):** the RB-cliff causation question (biological vs. survivorship vs. usage) and the future-pick discount rate rest on different defensible assumptions and cannot be adjudicated by current public data.
- **Empirical gaps:** there is no public, rigorous study of dynasty rebuild success rates or championship-roster age; strategic writing on contend-vs-rebuild is largely theory and anecdote. Treat confident claims here skeptically.
- **Source-quality flags:** many "peak age" numbers come from content-mill or low-transparency sources (undisclosed methodology, small samples, era-mixing). The most rigorous named work is Harstad (Footballguys), Fantasy Points, RotoViz, and the imported baseball/hockey methods literature (Tango, Lichtman, Turtoro, EvolvingWild). Massey-Thaler is peer-reviewed but its dynasty transfer is analogical, not tested.
- **Survivorship bias afflicts nearly every aging study cited**, including the orthodox ones — so the "cliff" magnitude is probably overstated in raw curves, which is the skeptics' central and largely conceded point.
- **Reflexivity means crowdsourced values partly create the reality they measure**, so "the market says X" is weaker evidence than it appears for KTC specifically.
- Player/tool specifics (trade counts, exact coefficients) drift over time; the DynastyProcess formula and 80% future-pick rule reflect its published methodology as last documented and may have been updated.
